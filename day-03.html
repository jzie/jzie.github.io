Day 3, 19 September 2026

Deciding what counts as a yes

Goal for the session: turn the POC question into criteria that can pass or fail, before any testing begins, so the answer cannot drift to match whatever the prototype happens to do.

Phase
3 of 10: criteria, then custom vehicle values
Kind of session
Product work, no code
Outcome
13 criteria, 4 must-pass, and a new phase

What I did, and why

  1. Drafted twelve candidate criteria and chose what to keep.Why: A POC without pass-or-fail criteria cannot produce an answer, only an impression. I kept nine as proposed, split the extension test in two, added a complete ride log, and kept cost and the older motorcycle as later measures.
  2. Picked the must-pass criteria: warning lights at key-turn, a usable cluster after key-turn, the safety split surviving an Android crash, and read-only on the bike.Why: Must-pass criteria separate a no from a not yet. If one of these fails, the answer is no whatever else passes.
  3. Set a threshold for every criterion, with the stock R3 as the baseline for timing.Why: Numbers set before testing cannot be bent to fit the results, and at least as good as the original is a bar nobody can argue with.
  4. Defined how freshness is measured: slow-motion video of the stock cluster and my display side by side, 19 of 20 neutral-to-first shifts within 200 ms.Why: A threshold you cannot measure is a wish. Video measures what the rider sees, including the display's own delay.
  5. Built an acceptance criteria page that keeps every test result for every criterion.Why: A single status hides regressions. Showing when a criterion first passed, and whether it has passed ever since, makes a fix that later broke visible at a glance.
  6. Added Phase 7, a safety layer for the warning lights, independent of Android.Why: Laying the criteria against the roadmap showed two must-pass criteria with no phase able to test them. A plan that cannot answer its own question needs changing, not the criteria.

Evidence

The must-pass criteria

#Pass if
1Warning lights visible within 1 second of key-on, no slower than the stock R3
2Live speed and RPM within 10 seconds of key-on
5Warning lights unaffected across 10 forced Android crashes
8Zero frames sent to the bike, confirmed by a second sniffer

All thirteen, with their history, are on the acceptance criteria page.

Two criteria in tension

A 10-second start-up almost certainly means keeping Android asleep rather than fully off. But the R3's 7 Ah battery would lose half its charge in about a month at 5 mA in total, which is why our hardware may add at most 1 mA.

A car can afford that standby. This motorcycle probably cannot, and the POC will show which criterion gives.

What I learned

Criteria change the plan

Writing the criteria exposed a phase the plan had never included. The criteria were right and the plan was incomplete, which is exactly what criteria are for.

The method defines the number

200 ms measured by software timestamps and 200 ms measured on video are different claims. Choosing the method is part of choosing the threshold.

Baselines beat absolute targets

No slower than the original cluster is easier to defend than any number picked in isolation, and the original is sitting in my garage.

The small battery is a real constraint

Fast start-up and low standby drain pull against each other much harder on a motorcycle than in a car. It may be the sharpest difference found so far.

Decisions made today

  • Pass-or-fail criteria, with a must-pass subset
  • Thresholds set before any testing
  • The stock R3 as the baseline
  • Measure freshness by slow-motion video
  • Gear position and tyre pressure as the extension tests
  • Log every frame on the bus
  • Track cost rather than gate on it
  • A dedicated phase for the safety layer
