About me

I came to product the long way round: twelve years as a software engineer in Java, C++ and Python, then six as a product owner. That's why I tend to ask "but how would we actually build this?" early in a meeting. I'm told this is annoying at the time and useful later.

Today I own IoT connectivity and mobile apps at a global motorcycle manufacturer, covering the roadmap for five hardware families and the companion app on Android and iOS, together good for 151,000 units in two and a half years. That has meant shipping things riders actually touch: ride modes, electronic suspension settings, audio controls, turn-by-turn navigation, and a track app that logs ten riding data points so people can chase their own lap times. It has also meant less glamorous wins I'm just as proud of, like a firmware update monitoring system that cut failed updates by 70%.

Before that I built an API gateway platform from zero to one at a global fashion group: the vision, the team, and the technical foundations that eleven applications now depend on, plus in-store apps wired into RFID readers and till systems that gave staff back over an hour a day. Earlier still, a sportswear manufacturer, a commerce consultancy in the UK, and my first years as a Java developer on a grocery site serving seven million people a month.

The thread through all of it: I learn by building. Right now that means finding out whether Android Automotive OS can run a motorcycle instrument cluster, with my own bike as the test bench and my evenings as the budget.

Projects

Can Android Automotive run a motorcycle instrument cluster?

Personal feasibility study, 2026, in progress

Android Automotive was designed for cars: two screens, a seated driver, a cabin. A motorcycle has one screen, a rider wearing gloves in the rain, and warning lights that are legally required to work the instant the key turns.

I'm building a working prototype fed by live data from a 2026 Yamaha R3 to find where the boundary sits between a safety layer and an Android layer, and whether what's left makes sense on a vehicle where every euro of the bill of materials is fought over.

It's also a deliberate test of my own habit: I spend my days making platform decisions for connected vehicles, so I wanted to know what those decisions feel like from underneath.

Where it stands: the app layer works end to end on an emulator. Next is reading the bike's own data. Every decision is recorded with its options and trade-offs, including the ones I got wrong.
